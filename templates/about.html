System Architecture & Viva Defense Guide

Technical methodology, deep learning computer vision pipeline, pharmacological knowledge representation, and viva examination preparation.

Problem Statement & Objectives

"Given an image of a plant part (leaf), identify the plant species, determine whether it is a recognized medicinal plant, and retrieve its documented medicinal uses, traditionally used plant parts, preparation information, dosage information where an authoritative source provides it, precautions, contraindications, and other relevant information."

Over 80% of the world's population relies on traditional herbal medicine for primary healthcare. However, visual misidentification of medicinal leaves and accidental poisoning with morphologically similar toxic plants (such as confusing Nerium oleander or Dieffenbachia with benign species) poses severe clinical hazards. Furthermore, anecdotal folklore frequently leads to incorrect preparation and dosage toxicity. This project solves these challenges through an end-to-end explainable AI system combining deep vision with authoritative pharmacopoeial monographs.

WHO & API Monographs

Authoritative Pharmacopoeial Sources

World Health Organization (WHO): Monographs on Selected Medicinal Plants (Volumes 1 through 4), Geneva. Provides international clinical efficacy, therapeutic dosages, and adverse reaction profiles.
Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India (API): Ministry of AYUSH, Government of India. Official statutory standard for traditional preparations (Swarasa, Kashayam, Churna) and classical therapeutic posology.
European Medicines Agency (EMA): Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products (HMPC) Community Herbal Monographs. Modern phytotherapeutic evaluation of clinical safety and herbal-drug interactions.
Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR): Monographs on Indian Medicinal Plants & Clinical Phytomedicine Guidelines.

Frequently Asked Viva Voce Questions & Answers

Exam Preparation

Q1: Why use MobileNetV3 rather than standard heavy CNNs like VGG-16?

Answer: MobileNetV3 incorporates depthwise separable convolutions, inverted bottleneck blocks with squeeze-and-excitation attention, and hard-swish activation functions. This reduces parameter count and computational complexity by over 75% compared to traditional CNNs (e.g. VGG or DenseNet), enabling sub-100ms real-time inference on edge devices, smartphones, and low-power clinical setups while retaining top-tier classification accuracy.

Q2: What is the role of Grad-CAM in this project?

Answer: Deep neural networks often function as opaque "black boxes." Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) computes the gradients of the predicted class score with respect to the feature activation maps of the final convolutional layer. By generating a 2D thermal visual heatmap overlaid onto the original leaf image, it proves whether the network made its diagnosis based on authentic botanical morphological cues (such as venation or leaf margins) rather than confounding background artifacts.

Q3: How does the system distinguish recognized medicinal plants from toxic ones?

Answer: The system maintains explicit negative and toxic control classes (such as Dieffenbachia seguine and Nerium oleander). The classifier assigns class probabilities, and the verification engine cross-references the predicted species with pharmacopoeial registries. If a toxic or non-medicinal plant is detected, the UI instantly activates high-priority red warning alerts, presents clinical poison center protocols, and disallows medicinal dosage instructions.

Q4: Why is leaf morphometry combined with deep learning?

Answer: Combining traditional OpenCV geometric feature extraction (aspect ratio, circularity, vein texture density) with deep CNN representations provides complementary verification. While CNNs capture complex non-linear textures, morphological metrics validate classical botanical taxonomy descriptions (e.g., distinguishing lanceolate from orbicular leaves).
